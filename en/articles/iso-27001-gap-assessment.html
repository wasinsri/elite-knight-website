ISO/IEC 27001 Gap Assessment: How Should Your Organization Prepare?

Turn a compliance gap review into a practical security roadmap connected to risk, business priorities, and executive decisions.

Published 28 August 2026 · 7 min read

LinkedInFacebookX

When customers, partners, or regulators ask how critical information is protected, a credible answer cannot stop at a list of cybersecurity tools. It must show risk management, accountable owners, operating controls, evidence, and continual improvement. ISO/IEC 27001 provides that structure through an information security management system, or ISMS.

A gap assessment shows the distance between current practices and the standard's expectations before the organization invests in remediation or seeks certification. The purpose is not to produce an attractive score; it is to identify which gaps create real risk and deserve priority.

Key takeaways

  • A badly drawn scope is the top reason a gap assessment wastes time and fails to answer executive questions.
  • Assess clauses 4 to 10 as well as all four Annex A themes. Annex A alone is not an assessment.
  • Rate against evidence actually seen, not against questionnaire answers.

What is an ISO/IEC 27001 gap assessment?

A gap assessment compares the organization's context, scope, policies, processes, risk assessment, controls, monitoring, and evidence against ISO/IEC 27001:2022 requirements, including the risk-based selection of Annex A controls. The result should distinguish what exists, what operates without sufficient evidence, what is missing, and the impact of leaving each gap unresolved.

Who should conduct a gap assessment?

The assessment is useful for organizations preparing for certification, responding to customer requirements, scaling digital services, consolidating fragmented controls, or strengthening cybersecurity governance before deciding on certification. Participants typically include executives, process owners, IT and security, risk, legal, HR, procurement, facilities, and internal audit as relevant to scope.

What are the six practical assessment steps?

Step 1: Define the objective and scope

State which services, processes, data, sites, cloud systems, suppliers, and interfaces sit inside the ISMS, with the reason for each inclusion or exclusion. A narrow scope that matches what customers actually buy is worth more than a broad one that never finishes.

Step 2: Collect documents and evidence

Gather policies, the risk register, asset inventory, access reviews, incident records, backup tests, vendor reviews, training records, and monitoring reports before any interview. This step usually reveals controls that exist on paper with no record of being performed.

Step 3: Interview and observe actual work

Verify that what is written actually happens: who decides, how often, and where the evidence is kept. The most effective question is to ask for the most recent example of the control being performed, not whether a procedure exists.

Step 4: Rate requirements and controls

Classify each item as in place, partial, absent, or not applicable with a stated reason. Rate against evidence actually seen rather than questionnaire answers, because inflated ratings resurface as findings in the certification audit.

Step 5: Prioritize by risk and dependency

Put gaps affecting critical data, legal obligations, customers, and business continuity first, and note which gaps must be closed before others can be, so teams do not run parallel work that blocks itself.

Step 6: Build the remediation roadmap

Name owners, targets, the evidence required, budget, timeline, quick wins, and the decisions that need executive escalation. A good roadmap states when, once completed, the organization will be ready for certification.

Which gaps are most commonly found?

  • An unclear ISMS scope or missing critical dependencies.
  • A risk assessment disconnected from business risk and without acceptance criteria.
  • Asset inventories that omit information owners, cloud services, or suppliers.
  • Security tools exist, but reviews, testing, and evidence of effectiveness are missing.
  • Incident response, backup, and continuity plans exist but have never been exercised together.
  • Supplier security is checked before contracting but not monitored through the service lifecycle.

What should executives receive from the assessment?

A useful report should be more than a checklist with hundreds of rows. Executives need an executive summary, risk heatmap, decision points, quick wins, medium-term initiatives, dependencies, indicative investment, and certification readiness. Operating teams need findings linked to requirements, reviewed evidence, accountable owners, and clear closure criteria.

Which mistakes should be avoided?

  • Copying policies or a Statement of Applicability from another organization without linking controls to actual risks.
  • Leaving the work entirely to IT without information and business process owners.
  • Measuring document existence instead of whether controls operate and achieve intended outcomes.
  • Trying to close every gap at once without considering risk, resources, or dependencies.

A Thai scenario: a scope too broad to finish

A Thai software vendor was asked by an enterprise customer for ISO/IEC 27001 certification within a year. The team scoped the whole company, thinking it looked more credible. The gap assessment returned 180 findings, well beyond what a team of twelve could handle.

The second pass narrowed the scope to the SaaS product the customer used, the engineering and operations teams behind it, and the cloud region serving it. Findings fell to 64 while still satisfying the customer, because the scope covered what they actually bought. A narrow, accurate scope beats a broad one you cannot finish.

Conclusion

A valuable ISO/IEC 27001 gap assessment is not a pass-or-fail exercise. It helps leaders see which information and services matter, where risks sit, which controls work, and what investment comes next. Clear scope, evidence-based review, and risk-based prioritization turn the standard from a documentation project into a sustainable digital trust management system.

Frequently asked questions

What is an ISO/IEC 27001 gap assessment?

It compares the current ISMS with the standard to identify gaps, risks, missing evidence, and remediation priorities.

How is it different from a certification audit?

A gap assessment prepares and guides remediation; a certification audit is a formal assessment by an independent certification body.

Must every system be assessed?

Not necessarily, but the scope must align with the services, processes, information, and risks being managed, with interfaces clearly justified.

What should the deliverables include?

Readiness levels, evidence-based findings, risks, owners, quick wins, and a decision-ready remediation roadmap.

How long does a gap assessment take?

For a mid-sized organization with a clear scope, four to eight weeks is typical: about two weeks for scoping and document collection, two to four weeks for interviews and evidence review, and one to two weeks for the report and roadmap. An over-broad scope is the main reason it stretches into months.
