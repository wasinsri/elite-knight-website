Data Quality Issues: Fix the Root Cause

A Data Governance approach for Thai enterprises to assign data issue owners, prioritize business impact, and verify fixes before closure.

27 November 2026 · 8 min read

LinkedInFacebookX

Key takeaways

  • Assign ownership, assess impact, and close only after user verification.

On this page

  1. Give data problems a clear route
  2. Report impact rather than blame
  3. Prioritize the affected business activity
  4. Separate accountability from repair work
  5. Contain the impact and correct the cause
  6. Close with evidence and watch recurrence

Give data problems a clear route

Conflicting sales reports, duplicate customer codes, and missing delivery fields are often corrected in downstream spreadsheets. The same problem returns in the next reporting cycle because nobody follows it from origin to business impact. Data Governance needs a shared route for data issues: receive the report, assess impact, assign action, and verify the outcome. This article proposes a practical starting process for one critical dataset in a Thai enterprise. Keep decisions traceable without making the reporting process so burdensome that users avoid it. The goal is to sustain business operations while correcting the conditions that repeatedly produce unreliable data.

Report impact rather than blame

A useful intake form asks what the user was trying to do, where the anomaly appeared, when it began, and what suitable evidence is available. Separate observations from hypotheses: different totals are an observation; a faulty sales-system export is a hypothesis until investigated. Prefer record references or identifiers over unnecessarily circulating full customer datasets. Note whether a temporary workaround exists and who is affected. This gives the receiving team enough context to reproduce the problem without repeated meetings. Do not require reporters to complete a technical root-cause analysis before submitting an issue. That requirement can hide important defects until they affect customers or decisions.

Prioritize the affected business activity

Assess effects on services, decisions, and the time remaining before the data is needed, rather than counting faulty rows alone. One incorrect record may block an important order, while hundreds of formatting differences may have little operational effect. Data and process owners should agree urgency, acknowledgement expectations, and update intervals. A starting classification might distinguish blocked work, work continuing through a workaround, and routine improvement, but adapt it to the organization. Where impact is uncertain, record that assessment is pending and assign further investigation. Uncertainty is not evidence that an issue is minor. Revisit priority when new affected users, dependencies, or deadlines become visible.

Separate accountability from repair work

Make the data owner accountable for definitions, quality rules, and acceptance of business consequences. A data steward can coordinate evidence and follow-up, while an application or operations team performs the repair. Give each issue one accountable coordinator to prevent circular handoffs, without expecting that person to do every task. For cross-department issues, define escalation and authority over disputed definitions or resources. A request to change a metric's meaning may require a data-rule change decision rather than a developer quietly adjusting a formula. Record the rationale and approver so other report users understand the consequences. A clear owner supports collaboration; it should not become a mechanism for assigning blame.

Contain the impact and correct the cause

Correcting historical records may be necessary to keep work moving. Record the method, approval, and affected scope while investigating whether the origin is data entry, ambiguous rules, integration, or a manual workflow. Duplicate customer identifiers may arise from multiple intake channels that do not check existing records. Merging duplicates alone will not prevent recurrence. Select a preventive action linked to the cause and assess systems sharing the data. Use the organization's change approval process and an appropriate rollback method for production changes. Urgency does not remove the need to check dependencies. A corrected record in one system may otherwise be overwritten by an older value from another in the next cycle.

Close with evidence and watch recurrence

Before closure, have a user or process owner validate sample transactions, compare them with agreed quality rules, and confirm that downstream reports receive correct data. Distinguish impact contained from root cause resolved, so a workaround is not counted as a permanent repair. Keep test evidence, remaining limitations, and the accepting owner together. Observe subsequent data cycles according to business criticality. Review resolution time, ageing backlog, and recurrence together instead of rewarding closed-ticket counts alone. Pilot the process on one dataset and use actual cases to improve intake and decisions. A Data Governance consultant should provide reusable roles, prioritization criteria, examples of closure evidence, and a recurrence review method that the internal team can continue.

Frequently asked questions

Who should receive data issue reports?

Use an accessible common route with a coordinator able to assign investigation. Reporters should not need to identify the originating system in advance.

Does every issue need committee review?

No. Teams should act within agreed authority, escalating cross-functional conflicts, important definition changes, or consequences beyond that authority.

Can correcting a report close the issue?

Check whether the defect is confined to that report or originates upstream. Record a workaround separately from permanent root-cause resolution.

How should success be measured?

Combine business impact, resolution time, backlog age, and recurrence, with user-verified outcomes. Closed issue counts alone can reward superficial fixes.

How can a team begin with little overhead?

Choose one critical dataset, use existing tools, and agree owners and minimum evidence. Improve the process using real cases before extending it enterprise-wide.
