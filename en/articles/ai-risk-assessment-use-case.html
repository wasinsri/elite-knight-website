What Is an AI Risk Assessment? Evaluate Use Cases Before Deployment

How to turn AI assessment from a compliance document into a decision process that enables responsible innovation.

Published 20 September 2026 · 7 min read

LinkedInFacebookX

An AI tool that summarizes internal documents does not carry the same risk as one that screens applicants, recommends credit limits, or supports health decisions. A single process can delay low-risk work and under-review critical work. AI risk assessment calibrates controls to actual impact.

Key takeaways

  • Assessment depth should scale with impact on people, not with technical complexity.
  • Use ISO/IEC 23894 and the NIST AI RMF for process structure, and ISO/IEC 42001 when an auditable management system is needed.
  • Define stop conditions before launch, because deciding to stop after an incident is usually too late.

What is an AI risk assessment?

It is a process for identifying, analyzing, and managing risk throughout an AI use case lifecycle: problem and data selection, development or procurement, testing, deployment, monitoring, and retirement. The goal is not zero risk, but informed decisions, proportionate controls, and explicit acceptance of residual risk.

Where should the assessment start? With impact on people and the business

Ask how AI affects rights, opportunities, safety, privacy, reputation, or financial performance, and how meaningfully people can review or correct its outputs. This grounds the conversation in business terms and clarifies risk ownership.

Which risk dimensions must be reviewed?

Dimension 1: Data quality and fitness

Check that data is complete, accurate, current, and used for the purpose it was collected for, and that training data reflects the population the system will actually serve. A mismatch here becomes a systematic error that is hard to unwind later.

Dimension 2: Fairness and bias

Test whether outcomes differ materially between groups and whether any difference is explainable on business grounds. Agree the acceptable difference threshold in advance rather than interpreting it after seeing the results.

Dimension 3: Privacy and security

Assess whether data or models could leak, be accessed beyond permission, or be attacked, covering training data, runtime data, and the traces left in logs, which are routinely overlooked.

Dimension 4: Reliability and robustness

Check that outputs stay accurate when context shifts, inputs are malformed, or someone tries to game the system. For language-based systems, always run prompt-injection tests before launch.

Dimension 5: Transparency and governance

Confirm that users understand the system limits, that decisions are evidenced, and that there is an escalation path when an output looks wrong, including who may review or overturn it.

How should use cases be tiered so approval stays fast and deep enough?

Use three simple tiers: low for assistive work without consequential decisions; medium for internal-data or business-process use; and high for systems affecting people, finances, safety, or regulatory compliance. High-risk use cases should have a business owner, impact assessment, pre-deployment testing, human oversight, and clear approval authority.

What evidence is required before deployment?

Retain the purpose, owner, data sources, provider or model, test results, limitations, controls, approver, and monitoring plan. It need not be lengthy, but must support review and decision-making when questions or incidents arise.

What must continue after go-live?

Data, user behavior, and providers change. Define performance indicators, reportable events, review triggers, and stop conditions. Monitoring makes risk assessment a management cycle rather than a one-time form.

A Thai scenario: a ranking system that learned yesterday's bias

A company deployed an application-ranking system to cut screening from two weeks to two days. It learned from five years of hiring history and scored each applicant.

Group-level testing before launch showed applicants from institutions outside Bangkok scored systematically lower. The cause was historical hiring patterns in the training data rather than capability. The team removed the institution variable, reduced the system to a ranking suggestion for human decision, and required that anyone filtered out by the system still get one human pass.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI risk assessment?

It evaluates the impact and likelihood of risk from an AI use case so decisions and controls can match its risk level.

Must every use case follow the same process?

No. Low-risk work should move quickly, while use cases affecting people, rights, opportunities, or important decisions need deeper review.

Which standard should frame the assessment process?

ISO/IEC 23894 gives AI risk management guidance built on ISO 31000, while the NIST AI Risk Management Framework offers four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — that translate readily into procedure. Where a certifiable management system is wanted, ISO/IEC 42001 sits above both.

How long should assessing one use case take?

Low risk should close in two to three working days with a one-page screen. Medium takes two to three weeks including testing. High usually runs six to ten weeks because of fairness testing and oversight design. If low-risk cases take more than a week, the screening process is too heavy.

Is reassessment needed when the model changes?

Reassess when the vendor or major model version changes, when data sources change, or when use expands to a new user group. For automatic minor updates from the vendor, run scheduled regression testing rather than a full reassessment each time.
