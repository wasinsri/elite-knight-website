PDPA and Generative AI: Using Enterprise Data Responsibly

A practical framework for Thai organizations seeking value from generative AI while protecting privacy, security, and trust.

Published 20 September 2026 · 7 min read

LinkedInFacebookX

Generative AI can accelerate document summaries, customer responses, knowledge retrieval, and drafting. It can also move sensitive information outside normal processes. The central question is not only which tool to use, but which data may enter it, under what conditions, and who is accountable.

Key takeaways

  • PDPA is not a final checkpoint. It is a design input for the use case from day one.
  • Fix the lawful basis under section 24 or 26 before starting, and never assume data already held can serve a new purpose automatically.
  • Most AI vendors act as data processors, so a complete section 40 processing agreement is required.

When does PDPA enter the picture? At use-case design, not at the finish line

When a use case involves personal data, define its purpose, test necessity, establish the appropriate lawful basis, and communicate transparently with data subjects. Data collected for one purpose is not automatically suitable for every new AI purpose.

Lawful bases that actually fit each type of Generative AI use case
Use case typeTypical lawful basisKey condition
An internal knowledge assistant limited to policy and manual documentsNot personal data, so no PDPA basis is needed.Confirm the corpus contains no names, ID numbers, or contact details.
Summarising customer conversation logs for the service teamLegitimate interest under section 24(5), or contractual necessity under section 24(3).Run and record a balancing test, and state the purpose in the privacy notice.
Screening or ranking job applicantsConsent or pre-contractual necessity under section 24(3); section 26 applies if sensitive data is involved.Provide human review and a route for applicants to contest the outcome.

Why classify data before selecting controls?

Use an actionable classification: public, internal, confidential business, and personal or sensitive data. Define what must never enter public tools, what can use only an approved environment, and when data must be minimized, pseudonymized, or de-identified.

Which questions must be answered before approving a use case?

What business outcome is sought, and is there a less data-intensive alternative?

If there is no answer to how success will be measured, the case is not ready. Often the same outcome can be reached by changing a process or using aggregated data that identifies no one, which removes the PDPA burden entirely.

What data will be submitted, processed, retained, or transferred?

Write the full data path from origin to deletion, including what rides along in attachments and conversation history. The most common leak is not the main database but what users paste into a prompt without thinking.

Is personal data truly necessary?

Test it by removing the identifying fields and asking whether the result still works. If it does, use masked or synthetic data instead. The PDPA principle of collecting only what is necessary is the cheapest risk control available.

Who owns the use case, the data, and the risk of the output?

Name individuals and roles, not departments, because when an output is wrong and affects a customer, someone must have the authority to switch it off without waiting for the next meeting.

Can the provider use data for training, and can retention be configured?

Check the contract terms and the actual product settings, not the marketing page. Get written answers on training use, processing location, log retention, and deletion rights.

If an answer is wrong, leaked, or biased, how will it be detected, stopped, and reported?

Detection must not rely solely on customer complaints; there must be a workable way to switch the feature off, and a reporting path that meets the statutory timeline for notifying a personal data breach.

What should vendor due diligence cover?

Before connecting data or scaling a tool, business, IT, security, legal, and privacy teams should jointly review provider roles, data-processing terms, cross-border transfers, encryption, access controls, logging, incident notification, and audit or exit rights.

How do you turn principles into daily behaviour?

A good policy tells people what they may do, must not do, and when to seek advice. Sustainable control also needs approved tools, configured access, role-based training, use-case screening, and a simple reporting path—not reliance on employee memory alone.

How do you start on a path you can control?

Choose a pilot with clear value and lower risk, such as summarizing public documents or drafting internal content without personal data. Build evidence from real use, then expand toward more complex use cases.

A Thai scenario: summarising call notes that carry health data

A customer service team at an insurer began feeding call notes into an automatic summarisation tool to cut after-call work from five minutes to one. Those notes contained names, policy numbers, and sometimes the policyholder's health information.

The problem was not summarisation. Health data is sensitive data under section 26, requiring explicit consent, and the tool was a public service with no section 40 agreement. The team's fix was to strip sensitive fields before sending, move to an enterprise contract with model training disabled, and limit summaries to service-handling points only.

What are the common mistakes, and how are they fixed?

Common mistakes and how to fix them
MistakeFix
Assuming data already collected can feed AI immediately, which is use beyond the purpose notified under section 27.Review the privacy notice and the section 39 processing record, and update both before the use case starts.
Banning AI without an approved alternative, so staff use personal tools out of sight.Roll out approved tooling alongside the policy, and say clearly what is permitted.
Letting knowledge-base permissions exceed the user's own rights, so the assistant surfaces documents they were never entitled to see.Enforce document-level permissions at the retrieval layer and test with real accounts at several levels before launch.

Frequently asked questions

Can personal data be entered into generative AI?

Evaluate purpose, necessity, lawful basis, notice, and controls first. Where data can be minimized or de-identified, choose that path first.

Is an AI policy alone enough?

No. It must be supported by use-case screening, data approval, provider configuration, user training, and incident monitoring.

Is a DPIA required for every use case?

Not in every case, but do one when the use case handles sensitive data under section 26, processes personal data at scale, involves systematic behavioural monitoring, or affects individual rights such as employment, credit, or service access. For internal use cases with no personal data, a short screening form is enough.

Is disabling the vendor's model-training feature enough?

It removes one risk only. You still need to check conversation retention, whether vendor staff can read content for abuse monitoring, where processing happens, and which subprocessors are involved. Training being off does not mean data is not stored.

Does the organization need a DPO for AI work?

The DPO requirement comes from section 41 and depends on the organization's activities, not on AI use as such. Where a DPO already exists, involve them at the use-case screening stage rather than close to launch, because redesigning later costs far more.
